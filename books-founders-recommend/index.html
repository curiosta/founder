Ideas from books

Books that well-known founders say they recommend

A hand-built sample with ties, not a league table. 32 people, 125 recommendations we could open, 104 books, checked on 26 Oct 2026.

What this page is, and what it is not

  • It is a sample we built by hand. We chose people who are widely known as founders or company builders, and counted a recommendation only where we opened a page showing them name the book. It is not a poll and not a bestseller list.
  • The data is flat. The most any book got is 4 people. 90 of the 104 books were named by one person each, so below the top 14 we cannot rank anything: they are tied.
  • A recommendation is not proof that the advice works. We only see books read by people whose firms did well (survivorship). A founder who names a book may be promoting a friend, a sponsor or their own book.
  • Many lists were given on request to a magazine or a TV series, so they are tidy and good for one’s image. Several picks are reported by a newspaper, not found in the founder’s own words; each name below says which.
  • Mostly Western. Indian names are fewer and come mostly from magazine lists. We did not check Indian or Hindi editions.
  • No affiliate links. Links to books go to Open Library, a free library catalogue, so you can check that the book exists. We earn nothing from any of them.

How we counted

Each person counts once per book, however many times they named it. Books are ranked by the number of different people. Books with the same number share a rank, shown with an equals sign (for example 2=). Warren Buffett is an investor, not a founder; we kept him, labelled. Three people from one 2014 YourStory list are less widely known (Raghunandan G, Suvir Sujan, Aprameya Radhakrishna). We kept them, labelled, and show each count both ways, written as “if the lesser-known are left out”. Good to Great, for example, is joint second only because two of its three names are lesser-known.

The 14 books named by two or more people

Ranked by the number of different people. Each entry gives the year, a free Open Library record, a description in our own words, and Our suggestion of which level of the ladder the book suits. The suggestion is our reading, not the founders’.

  1. 1 The Innovator's Dilemma

    Clayton M. Christensen. First published 1997. Open Library record. Recommended by 4 people.

    Argues that well-run established firms can lose to cheaper, simpler products that start at the low end or in new markets. Built around case studies of industries such as disk drives.

    Our suggestion Level 4 (new products via technology). It frames how a small newcomer can beat a large incumbent, which is the Level 4 question. Read the Lepore critique first.

    Who named it, and where
    • Marc Andreessen: Quartz (Alice Truong), 'The essential Marc Andreessen summer reading list' (quotes his tweets via highlyreco.com; updated 2022-07-21), 21 Jul 2022 (updated; tweet dates not given) (as reported; Reported by Quartz from his tweets; the tweets were not opened.)
    • Mark Cuban: Inc. (Melanie Curtin), 'Mark Cuban Recommends These 5 Books for Entrepreneurs' (quotes Cuban), 27 Dec 2016 (as reported; Quoted by Inc. and Business Insider; neither names the original interview, and we did not open one.)
    • Michael Bloomberg: Business Insider (Gus Lubin, Alexandra Mondalek), '27 Business Leaders Name Their Favorite Books Ever', 2 Aug 2013 (as reported; Business Insider relays interviews given elsewhere; the originals were not opened.)
    • Steve Jobs: Business Insider, quoting Jobs's own public remark, 24 Feb 2021 (as reported; Business Insider quotes his words as recorded in Isaacson's biography; we opened neither the biography nor an original.)

    Contested Jill Lepore, a Harvard historian writing in The New Yorker in 2014, calls the book's cases handpicked. She says a fund Christensen launched in 2000 on his theory was wound up within a year after losing 64 per cent while the Nasdaq lost 50 per cent, and that disruption “can reliably be seen only after the fact”. We opened no reply from Christensen or his supporters, so this is one side. Source: The New Yorker (Jill Lepore), 'The Disruption Machine', 16 Jun 2014 (online; 23 June issue).

  2. 2= Business Adventures

    John Brooks. First published 1969. Open Library record. Recommended by 3 people.

    A collection of long-form magazine stories about real episodes in American business and finance, told through the people involved. It is about judgement and mistakes rather than a formula.

    Our suggestion Level 2 (Dealer) and 3. Short case histories of how real firms handled crises and growth, useful for owners running a business day to day.

    Who named it, and where
    • Bill Gates: Gates Notes (own blog), 'The best business book I've ever read', 13 Jul 2014 (own words)
    • Ritesh Agarwal: YourStory (Debolina Biswas), 'Drive into the New Year with Ritesh Agarwal' (embeds his tweet of 2020-12-12), 9 Jan 2021 (own words; His tweet as quoted by YourStory; the tweet was not opened.)
    • Warren Buffett (investor, not a founder): Gates Notes (Gates quoting Buffett), 13 Jul 2014 (as reported; Per Gates's account on the same page, so not independent of Gates.)

    Gates and Buffett are not independent here: Buffett’s pick comes from Gates’s own account. Counting them once, two independent sources name this book.

  3. 2= Good to Great

    Jim Collins. First published 2001. Open Library record. Recommended by 3 people (1 if the lesser-known are left out).

    Collins and his team compared eleven companies that sustained strong results with similar firms that did not, and extracted common traits such as disciplined people and focus. The method is backward-looking and its conclusions have been challenged.

    Our suggestion Level 3. Read for focus and discipline ideas for a growing firm, but treat as hypotheses, not proof (see critiques).

    Who named it, and where
    • Ken Griffin (hedge-fund founder, investor): Business Insider (Katherine Li), 'Summer reading list ... according to top CEOs', 4 Jul 2025 (as reported; Reported by Business Insider; the original interview was not opened.)
    • Raghunandan G (lesser-known): YourStory (Aditya Bhushan Dwivedi), 'This is what top VCs and entrepreneurs read in 2014 and recommend', 30 Dec 2014 (as reported; A list given to YourStory in 2014.)
    • Suvir Sujan (venture investor, lesser-known): YourStory (Aditya Bhushan Dwivedi), 'This is what top VCs and entrepreneurs read in 2014 and recommend', 30 Dec 2014 (as reported; A list given to YourStory in 2014; the paraphrase is YourStory's.)

    Contested Steven Levitt, an economist, wrote on the Freakonomics blog in 2008 that two of the eleven firms in the book were in trouble (Fannie Mae looked headed for a federal rescue; Circuit City's stock was down about 80 per cent since the book). He said nine of the eleven were still more or less intact and that a portfolio of all eleven looked likely to have underperformed the S&P 500. It is a short blog post, not a study. Source: Freakonomics (Steven Levitt), 'From Good to Great ... to Below Average', 28 Jul 2008.

  4. 2= The Hard Thing About Hard Things

    Ben Horowitz. First published 2014. Open Library record. Recommended by 3 people (2 if the lesser-known are left out).

    A venture capitalist and former software CEO describes the difficult people and cash decisions that textbooks skip. It is part memoir, part advice for the CEO's lonely moments.

    Our suggestion Level 3 (Manufacturer). Useful once there is a team and payroll to carry, when hard decisions about people and cash pile up.

    Who named it, and where
    • Marc Andreessen: Quartz (Alice Truong), 'The essential Marc Andreessen summer reading list' (quotes his tweets via highlyreco.com; updated 2022-07-21), 21 Jul 2022 (updated; tweet dates not given) (as reported; Reported by Quartz from his tweets; the tweets were not opened.)
    • Namita Thapar: Times of India web story, 'Shark Tank India's Namita Thapar's favourite books' (quotes her social posts), 23 Jan 2023 (as reported; Reported by the Times of India as her own book was being promoted; her posts were not opened.)
    • Raghunandan G (lesser-known): YourStory (Aditya Bhushan Dwivedi), 'This is what top VCs and entrepreneurs read in 2014 and recommend', 30 Dec 2014 (as reported; A list given to YourStory in 2014.)

  5. 2= Zero to One

    Peter Thiel with Blake Masters. First published 2014. Open Library record. Recommended by 3 people (2 if the lesser-known are left out).

    Built from Thiel's Stanford startup lecture notes, it argues that the best companies create something new rather than copy what exists. It is opinionated and makes bold claims about monopoly and competition.

    Our suggestion Level 4. Its theme is building something new rather than copying, which fits Level 4. The authors' views on monopoly are contested.

    Who named it, and where
    • Elon Musk: CNBC Make It, 'Billionaire Elon Musk credits his success to these 8 books' (Marguerite Ward), 21 Feb 2017 (as reported; One pick is in his own words (a 2012 interview); others are CNBC's reports. He and Peter Thiel co-founded PayPal, so they are not independent of each other.)
    • Raghunandan G (lesser-known): YourStory (Aditya Bhushan Dwivedi), 'This is what top VCs and entrepreneurs read in 2014 and recommend', 30 Dec 2014 (as reported; A list given to YourStory in 2014.)
    • Sam Altman: Hacker News thread transcribing Altman's AMA answers (Altman, as 'sama', confirms corrections in-thread), 21 Oct 2015 (as reported; Answers in a Q&A, transcribed on Hacker News; he confirmed corrections there.)

  6. 6= Creativity, Inc.

    Ed Catmull with Amy Wallace. First published 2014. Open Library record. Recommended by 2 people.

    Pixar's co-founder describes how the studio tried to build a culture where candid feedback and creative risk were possible. It mixes company history with management lessons.

    Our suggestion Level 3 / 4. Ideas on team culture and feedback for firms making something new.

    Who named it, and where
    • Mark Zuckerberg: Business Insider (Richard Feloni and Chris Weller), '22 books Mark Zuckerberg thinks everyone should read', quoting his Facebook posts, 17 Dec 2015 (as reported; Business Insider quotes his Facebook posts. A book-club pick, not a business-advice endorsement.)
    • Namita Thapar: Times of India web story, 'Shark Tank India's Namita Thapar's favourite books' (quotes her social posts), 23 Jan 2023 (as reported; Reported by the Times of India as her own book was being promoted; her posts were not opened.)

  7. 6= High Output Management

    Andrew S. Grove. First published 1983. Open Library record. Recommended by 2 people.

    A former Intel chief explains management as producing output through a team, with practical ideas on meetings, delegation and measuring work. It reads like a manual.

    Our suggestion Level 3. Practical management routines for an owner running a team or factory.

    Who named it, and where
    • Brian Chesky: The Washington Post (Jena McGregor), 'How a business book from the '80s became a cult classic in Silicon Valley'; also Business Insider (Katherine Li), 2025-07-04, 18 Nov 2015 (as reported; Reported by two outlets; his own words were not opened.)
    • Marc Andreessen: Quartz (Alice Truong), 'The essential Marc Andreessen summer reading list' (quotes his tweets via highlyreco.com; updated 2022-07-21), 21 Jul 2022 (updated; tweet dates not given) (as reported; Reported by Quartz from his tweets; the tweets were not opened.)

  8. 6= Originals

    Adam Grant. First published 2016. Open Library record. Recommended by 2 people.

    A psychologist looks at how people come up with and push new ideas, and argues that originals are not always the biggest risk-takers. It draws on research and anecdotes.

    Our suggestion Level 4. Covers how to judge and promote new ideas.

    Who named it, and where
    • Namita Thapar: Times of India web story, 'Shark Tank India's Namita Thapar's favourite books' (quotes her social posts), 23 Jan 2023 (as reported; Reported by the Times of India as her own book was being promoted; her posts were not opened.)
    • Whitney Wolfe Herd: Business Insider (Katherine Li), 4 Jul 2025 (as reported; Reported by Business Insider; the podcast was not opened.)

  9. 6= Sapiens

    Yuval Noah Harari. First published 2011. Open Library record. Recommended by 2 people.

    A broad history of humans, from early hunter-gatherers to modern states and markets. It argues that shared stories such as money and companies let large groups cooperate.

    Our suggestion Level 1 (Trader), as background. General background on why trade and trust exist; not a business how-to.

    Who named it, and where
    • Mark Zuckerberg: Business Insider (Richard Feloni and Chris Weller), '22 books Mark Zuckerberg thinks everyone should read', quoting his Facebook posts, 17 Dec 2015 (own words; Business Insider quotes his Facebook posts. A book-club pick, not a business-advice endorsement.)
    • Naval Ravikant: Almanack of Naval Ravikant, 'Naval's Recommended Reading' (Eric Jorgenson compilation of Naval's own words, updated 2024), 2024 (own words; From a site compiled by a third party that also promotes the Almanack book.)

  10. 6= The Better Angels of Our Nature

    Steven Pinker. First published 2011. Open Library record. Recommended by 2 people.

    Argues, using historical data, that violence has declined over centuries and explores why. It is a social-science book rather than a business book.

    Our suggestion Not level-specific. Background reading; no direct business use.

    Who named it, and where
    • Bill Gates: Business Insider (Shana Lebowitz, Marguerite Ward), '32 books Bill Gates thinks everyone should read' (quotes his Gates Notes reviews), 22 Dec 2020 (as reported)
    • Mark Zuckerberg: Business Insider (Richard Feloni and Chris Weller), '22 books Mark Zuckerberg thinks everyone should read', quoting his Facebook posts, 17 Dec 2015 (as reported; Business Insider quotes his Facebook posts. A book-club pick, not a business-advice endorsement.)

  11. 6= The Checklist Manifesto

    Atul Gawande. First published 2010. Open Library record. Recommended by 2 people (1 if the lesser-known are left out).

    A surgeon argues that simple checklists reduce errors in complex work such as surgery and aviation. It shows how to build and use them.

    Our suggestion Level 2 / 3. Checklists suit repeatable work in a shop or factory.

    Who named it, and where
    • Radhika Gupta (asset-manager CEO): CNBC-TV18 'Bookmark' series, written by Radhika Gupta (Edelweiss AMC MD & CEO), 'five favourites that influenced her beliefs', 22 Sep 2020 (own words; She wrote the list herself for a CNBC-TV18 series where leaders list books on request.)
    • Suvir Sujan (venture investor, lesser-known): YourStory (Aditya Bhushan Dwivedi), 'This is what top VCs and entrepreneurs read in 2014 and recommend', 30 Dec 2014 (as reported; A list given to YourStory in 2014; the paraphrase is YourStory's.)

  12. 6= The Rational Optimist

    Matt Ridley. First published 2010. Open Library record. Recommended by 2 people.

    Argues that trade and the exchange of ideas between specialists drove human progress. It takes an optimistic view of markets and technology.

    Our suggestion Level 1 (Trader). It makes the case for trade and specialisation, the base of the ladder. The optimistic stance is one side of a debate.

    Who named it, and where
    • Mark Zuckerberg: Business Insider (Richard Feloni and Chris Weller), '22 books Mark Zuckerberg thinks everyone should read', quoting his Facebook posts, 17 Dec 2015 (as reported; Business Insider quotes his Facebook posts. A book-club pick, not a business-advice endorsement.)
    • Naval Ravikant: Almanack of Naval Ravikant, 'Naval's Recommended Reading', 2024 (own words; From a site compiled by a third party that also promotes the Almanack book.)

  13. 6= What It Takes

    Stephen A. Schwarzman. First published 2019. Open Library record. Recommended by 2 people.

    The co-founder of Blackstone shares his career story and rules of thumb for building a firm. Written by a founder with a personal viewpoint.

    Our suggestion Level 4 / general. A founder memoir; reflects one person's view.

    Who named it, and where
    • Namita Thapar: Times of India web story, 'Shark Tank India's Namita Thapar's favourite books' (quotes her social posts), 23 Jan 2023 (as reported; Reported by the Times of India as her own book was being promoted; her posts were not opened.)
    • Nandan Nilekani: McKinsey & Company, 'We know what you are reading this summer' (2020 list), Nandan Nilekani entry, 2020 (summer reading list; exact day not shown on page) (own words)

  14. 6= When Genius Failed

    Roger Lowenstein. First published 2000. Open Library record. Recommended by 2 people.

    The story of Long-Term Capital Management, a hedge fund run by prominent traders and academics that collapsed in 1998 after heavy borrowing. It is a case study in leverage and overconfidence.

    Our suggestion Level 2 (Dealer). A warning on debt and risk, relevant to anyone trading with borrowed money.

    Who named it, and where
    • Nikhil Kamath: Business Today, 'Zerodha's Nikhil Kamath shares 7 books that shaped his thinking', 20 Jun 2026 (as reported; Reported by Business Today; his original post is not linked there.)
    • Radhika Gupta (asset-manager CEO): CNBC-TV18 'Bookmark' series, written by Radhika Gupta (Edelweiss AMC MD & CEO), 'five favourites that influenced her beliefs', 22 Sep 2020 (own words; She wrote the list herself for a CNBC-TV18 series where leaders list books on request.)

Six books named by one person each (unranked)

These six are tied with 84 other books at one person each, so they do not rank above any of them. We picked these six to describe because they suit the four levels of the ladder: that choice is ours.

  • Built to Last

    Jim Collins and Jerry I. Porras. First published 1994. Open Library record. Recommended by 1 person.

    Studies long-lived companies and compares them with rivals to identify shared habits such as strong core values. It uses the same comparison approach as Good to Great.

    Our suggestion Level 3. For founders thinking about a firm that outlasts them; same method caveats as Good to Great (by analogy only).

    Who named it, and where
    • Jeff Bezos: Business Insider (Gus Lubin, Alexandra Mondalek), '27 Business Leaders Name Their Favorite Books Ever', 2 Aug 2013 (as reported; Reported by Inc. and Business Insider; we opened none of the original interviews.)

    Contested Same author and method as Good to Great, so the same question applies. We opened no separate critique of this book, so we do not treat the Good to Great critique as proof against it.

  • Principles

    Ray Dalio. First published 2017. Open Library record. Recommended by 1 person.

    A hedge fund founder sets out his rules for life and management, including radical transparency at his firm. It is one person's system, applied in one firm.

    Our suggestion Level 3. Management rules for a growing team; treat as one approach among many.

    Who named it, and where
    • Deepinder Goyal: Times of India web story, 'Zomato CEO Deepinder Goyal's book recommendations' (about his Instagram post), 26 Mar 2024 (as reported; Times of India reports an Instagram post about copies bought for his team; the post was not opened.)

  • Shoe Dog

    Phil Knight. First published 2016. Open Library record. Recommended by 1 person.

    Nike's co-founder tells the early story of the company, from importing shoes to building a brand, including near-failures and money trouble. It is a personal memoir.

    Our suggestion Level 1 to 2. Starts as an importer and reseller, which is the trader-to-dealer path.

    Who named it, and where
    • Bill Gates: Gates Notes, 'My review of Phil Knight's memoir about Nike', 5 Dec 2016 (own words)

  • Start with Why

    Simon Sinek. First published 2009. Open Library record. Recommended by 1 person.

    Argues that organisations communicate and inspire best when they begin with their purpose rather than their product. It is popular for branding and leadership talks.

    Our suggestion Level 3 / 4. Helps clarify purpose and brand when you make your own products.

    Who named it, and where
    • Richard Branson: CNBC Make It (Tom Huddleston Jr.), list shared by Branson, 1 Jul 2024 (as reported; A list he gave to CNBC; he also sells his own books.)

  • The Everything Store

    Brad Stone. First published 2013. Open Library record. Recommended by 1 person.

    A journalist's account of the building of Amazon and the character of Jeff Bezos, based on interviews and reporting. It is not authorised by Amazon.

    Our suggestion Level 2 / 3. A case study of a dealer-style retailer that scaled logistics.

    Who named it, and where
    • Vijay Shekhar Sharma: YourStory (Aditya Bhushan Dwivedi), 'This is what top VCs and entrepreneurs read in 2014 and recommend', 30 Dec 2014 (as reported; A list given to YourStory in 2014.)

  • The Lean Startup

    Eric Ries. First published 2011. Open Library record. Recommended by 1 person.

    Proposes testing a business idea with small experiments and learning from customers before spending heavily. It popularised the idea of a minimum viable product.

    Our suggestion Level 4. A method for testing a new product cheaply.

    Who named it, and where
    • Reid Hoffman: Business Insider (Katherine Li), 4 Jul 2025 (as reported; Reported by Business Insider; he was on the author's own show.)

Years are from Open Library edition records. Its work-level date was wrong or missing for several books (Zero to One, Creativity, Inc., Originals, Shoe Dog), so we used the edition record. We did not check anything earlier, or Indian editions.

Two popular books that are contested

Two of the most recommended books have published critics. We opened these two pieces and show them on the book’s card above: Good to Great and The Innovator’s Dilemma. Built to Last uses the same method as Good to Great. Being criticised does not make a book useless; it means read it as a set of stories, not a proof.

Books already covered in Ideas from books

Our Ideas from books section covers Street Smarts and Eat People. We did not find either recommended by any source we opened for this page. That says nothing about their quality: they are simply not on this list.

Who is not covered, and why

We looked for Kunal Shah, Sridhar Vembu, Falguni Nayar, Peyush Bansal, Nithin Kamath, Bhavish Aggarwal, Ghazal Alagh, Aman Gupta, Paul Graham, Tim Ferriss's guests, Oprah. For each of them we found no page we could open that shows them naming a book, or saw them only in an aggregator or a search excerpt, which we did not count. So they are not covered, and a missing name is not a judgement. Kunal Shah, Sridhar Vembu and Falguni Nayar are the Indian founders you may expect to see; we simply could not source a pick from them. Sara Blakely appears only for an audio series, not a book, so she is not counted. Reid Hoffman appears for one book. We saw Stripe’s Collison and Charlie Munger’s Influence only in excerpts, so we left them out. We also left out chiefs of big firms on one Business Insider list because they are not founders.
