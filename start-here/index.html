A six-step route

Start here

New to this site, or to business? Follow these six steps in order, or jump to the one you need. Every step is a link to pages that are already here. The order is our suggestion, not a rule, and you can stop at any step.

  1. 1 Know your starting point

    Answer 14 questions, about three minutes. It runs in your browser and saves nothing.

    • Self-evaluation: which level do you want to build?
    • SOAR, a short tool for turning the result into a plan.

  2. 2 Pick your level

    Four levels, from buying and selling to making something new.

    • Level 1: Trader
    • Level 2: Dealer
    • Level 3: Manufacturer
    • Level 4: New products and services
    • How the levels connect, if you sit between two levels.

  3. 3 Learn the numbers

    Each level has a business-math section with worked examples. Start with the ones tagged Basic.

    • Trader: margins, GST, break-even, stock and cash
    • Dealer: stock finance, schemes, dealership return
    • Manufacturer: unit costing, machines, yield, pricing
    • New products: unit economics, market size, royalties

  4. 4 Think with a tool

    Frameworks are checklists for thinking, not answers.

    • Frameworks. Our suggestion Try SWOT and TOWS and PESTLE first, Five Forces next, and the rest later.
    • Plain words, for any business term that stops you.

  5. 5 Learn from others

    Books and habits of well-known founders, with the limits of that evidence stated on each page.

    • Ideas from books, in plain words.
    • Books that founders say they recommend
    • What well-known founders say they do each day
    • mind.curiosta.com, our sister site on how to learn, and how not to be fooled. It is a separate site.

  6. 6 Test small, then decide

    Our suggestion Write down what you expect. Run a small test. Write down what happened.

    • A decision article for your level: Trader, Dealer, Manufacturer, New products.
    • Calendar, for fairs and key dates.
    • Where this model breaks, the honesty check on our own model.

Building the habits and beliefs that help: what the research says

Many pages tell a new founder to “just believe in yourself”. We looked at what research we could open. Almost all of it studies students, workers or athletes, not first-time founders in India. We found none that tests these ideas on Indian founders.

What the sources we opened say

  • Growth mindset (the belief that ability can grow). Two meta-analyses, of 365,915 and 57,155 students, found “overall effects were weak”. Sisk and others, 2018. A short online lesson (under an hour) raised grades by 0.10 of a grade point for lower-achieving US ninth-graders, mostly where classmates backed challenge. Yeager and others, Nature, 2019.
  • Grit. It accounted for an average of 4% of the variance in success outcomes. Duckworth and others, 2007. A later review of 88 samples found it only moderately linked to performance and very strongly linked to conscientiousness. Credé and others, 2017.
  • Founders versus managers. Entrepreneurs scored a little higher on conscientiousness and openness and lower on neuroticism and agreeableness than managers. Each effect was small; taken together they were moderate. Zhao and Seibert, 2006.
  • Self-belief and intention. Among 265 MBA students, entrepreneurial self-efficacy sat between learning and experience on one side and the intention to start a business on the other. That is intention, not results. Zhao, Seibert and Hills, 2005.
  • Overconfidence. Of 2,994 new US business owners surveyed in 1985, 81% put their own odds of success at 7 out of 10 or better, and 33% said 10 out of 10. The poorly prepared were as optimistic as the well prepared. Cooper, Woo and Dunkelberg, 1988 (an open copy).
  • Practice. A 2019 attempt to repeat the best-known violin study did not reproduce its core finding, and the effect was smaller. Macnamara and Maitra, 2019.

Opinion or fashion, not research findings

  • “Believe and you will succeed.”
  • “Ten thousand hours makes an expert.”

We found these as slogans. We did not trace them to a source we opened, so we do not treat them as findings.

Our reading

Our suggestion Use the research as a reason to check, not to cheer. The Cooper result suggests new founders are already confident, so a habit of checking your own numbers and plans against what others actually got may help more than more belief. That is a suggestion, not something these studies tested on founders.

Manoj’s view The useful part of “mindset” for a beginner is a set of habits of checking: check your numbers, check your plan against others’ results, test small, and write down what failed. Habits of checking beat positive-thinking slogans. This is his opinion, not a finding.

Not covered: whether any of this holds for Indian first-time founders, and the wider debate on each study. Where a founder’s surroundings, such as credit, rules and markets, decide who survives, a mindset message cannot make up for it. Our model-limits page says where our own model is weak.
