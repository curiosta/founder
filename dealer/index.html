Level 2: Dealer

Becoming the authorised seller of a large brand in your area. In our view, more steady demand and less freedom.

Manoj's model The four levels are Manoj Sahukar's own way of thinking about business, not an established theory. Where this model breaks.

What this level is

A dealer is a trader who has tied up with a large manufacturing brand. Tyre dealerships, authorised auto-parts dealers, paint dealers, two-wheeler and tractor dealers, and electrical and appliance dealers all work at this level. The brand’s board over your shop tells customers that what you sell is genuine and that the company stands behind it.

Dealer or distributor? In standard usage, a distributor buys in bulk and sells on to other businesses (retailers, dealers, workshops), while a dealer sells to the final customer, usually under an authorisation from the brand. On this ladder, a multi-brand distributor or wholesaler of everyday goods sits at Level 1; what puts a business at Level 2 is a formal, brand-authorised relationship with a large manufacturer.

Where the profit comes from

  • The margin the brand allows: the gap between the brand’s price to you and the selling price, usually quoted as a percentage of the selling price.
  • Bonuses and schemes for meeting the brand’s sales targets.
  • Service income you add yourself: fitting, alignment, repair, installation, spares. This part is the most truly yours.

What it takes to climb to Level 3

  • Deep knowledge of the product, including what fails and what customers keep asking for.
  • Savings, and the discipline to run people and processes, not just a counter.
  • Often, a first small step into making: assembling, repairing or making one part you used to buy.

The sub-levels of level 2

Sub-levels are a first draft, being refined. A dealer sells one brand's products as its authorised partner. Within this level, businesses differ in how directly they deal with the brand, how much they do beyond selling (service and spares), and how many outlets or how much territory they run. Manoj's model The order is our own judgement of money needed, risk, control and value added, not a measured ranking. The words for money, risk and control are our judgement too, not sourced figures. Real businesses do not always climb in this order, and some do well by staying where they are. Where this model breaks.

  1. 2.1

    Sub-dealer

    You sell a brand's products in a smaller town or area, but you buy from the brand's authorised dealer or distributor, not from the brand itself. You usually have no direct agreement with the brand.

    Example: TVS Motor's dealer network has this shape: it describes an "authorised dealer" (AD) as a dealer of its "authorised main dealer" (AMD), usually in a town of under 1 lakh people. Similarly, a paint shop may buy from the brand's distributor rather than the brand.

    Money
    Small to moderate: a small outlet and some stock, far less than a main dealership.
    Risk
    Thin margins after the main dealer's share, and terms that the main dealer can change.
    Control
    Low: both the brand and the main dealer set your terms.
    To climb
    Build steady sales and a clean payment record, then apply to the brand for your own authorised dealership.
  2. 2.2

    Authorised dealer (sales)

    You have an agreement directly with the brand to sell its products in a territory, following its rules on display, prices, stock and targets.

    Example: An authorised tyre, paint, cement or consumer-durables dealer.

    Money
    Moderate: a showroom to the brand's standards, a security deposit, and the minimum stock the brand asks for.
    Risk
    Sales targets and scheme conditions, stock pushed on you to meet targets, and the brand's decisions on prices and new dealers nearby.
    Control
    Medium: you run the business, but the brand sets the rules.
    To climb
    Add service and genuine spares, where the brand allows it, to earn again from the customers you already have.

    Framework: Porter's Five Forces: check who holds the power over your margin

    Framework: NOISE: a quick weekly operations check

  3. 2.3

    3S dealer (sales, service, spares)

    You sell the brand's products, service them in your own workshop, and stock its genuine spare parts, all under one roof. "3S" stands for sales, service and spares. It is the usual format for cars, two-wheelers, tractors and commercial vehicles.

    Example: An authorised car or two-wheeler dealership with a showroom, a workshop and a spares counter.

    Money
    Large: showroom, workshop equipment, trained technicians and spares stock.
    Risk
    High fixed costs every month, workshop bays standing idle, and dependence on the brand's sales cycle.
    Control
    Medium, but service and spares bring income that does not depend only on new sales.
    To climb
    Run one outlet profitably and with good systems, then open more outlets or take on a second brand.
  4. 2.4

    Multi-outlet dealer group or multi-unit franchisee

    You run several dealerships or franchise outlets, of one brand or several, across towns or a state. (A franchisee runs an outlet under the brand's name and business format, in return for fees.)

    Example: A family business running dealerships of two vehicle brands in several districts; a franchisee running several outlets of one food brand.

    Money
    Very large: several showrooms and their stock, and a management team.
    Risk
    Loans and fixed costs across many outlets: a slowdown or one brand's decision hits them all at once.
    Control
    Higher: spreading across brands and towns reduces dependence on any one of them.
    To climb
    Build managers and systems that run outlets without you, then seek rights to a whole region.

    Framework: McKinsey 7-S: a readiness check before this climb

  5. 2.5

    Master franchisee or regional distributor

    A brand gives you the right to develop its business across a large region. A master franchisee can appoint, train and support sub-franchisees in that region; a regional distributor appoints and supplies the brand's dealers there.

    Example: A company holding the Indian master franchise of a foreign food brand and granting sub-franchises; a regional distributor appointing dealers for an appliance brand across several states.

    Money
    Very large: the fee for the rights, a regional team, and often outlets of your own to prove the format works.
    Risk
    Development targets you must meet, rights the brand may not renew, and responsibility for the whole network's problems.
    Control
    The most a dealer gets: you shape the brand's network in your region, within the brand's agreement.
    To climb
    The next step is a new level: make products yourself (Level 3) or sell something new of your own (Level 4).
