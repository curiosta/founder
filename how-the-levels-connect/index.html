How the levels connect

The four levels are not separate worlds. They are links in one chain, and these mind maps show how.

Think of any product you sell or use: a tyre, a packet of biscuits, a motor. Before it reaches the customer, it passes through many hands. Someone develops the idea or the technology. Someone makes it. Someone sells it under a brand through dealers. Someone else moves it in bulk to the shops. Each of these businesses earns its money at a different point along the way.

Management books call this the industry "value chain". In plain words: the chain of businesses a product passes through, from raw material to the final customer, with each one adding something and taking a share of the money. (Strictly, "value chain" on its own means the chain of activities inside a single company; the chain across companies is the industry value chain.)

The maps below use three kinds of line. Every box is a link: tap a sub-level to read about it on its level page. The customer boxes jump to where the customer's money goes.

2. Where does the money get split?

When a customer pays for something, that money is shared out along the chain. Each business keeps the difference between what it is paid and what it paid the step before. A trader or dealer uses that difference to pay its own rent, salaries and interest, and keeps the rest as profit. For the maker, the amount it receives must also pay for material and making, so its share is not comparable with a trader's margin.

The two maps below use made-up prices to show the arithmetic. Real margins vary widely by product, brand and region.

Hypothetical A ₹100 packet of biscuits

  • Kirana shop sells at ₹100 and buys at ₹85: ₹100 − ₹85 = ₹15. It keeps ₹15, which is ₹15 ÷ ₹100 × 100 = 15% of what the customer paid. Because it sells to the customer, this is also its margin on its own selling price.
  • Distributor sells at ₹85 and buys at ₹80: ₹85 − ₹80 = ₹5. It keeps ₹5, which is ₹5 ÷ ₹100 × 100 = 5% of what the customer paid. As a margin on its own selling price: ₹5 ÷ ₹85 × 100 = 5.88%.
  • Super stockist sells at ₹80 and buys at ₹76: ₹80 − ₹76 = ₹4. It keeps ₹4, which is ₹4 ÷ ₹100 × 100 = 4% of what the customer paid. As a margin on its own selling price: ₹4 ÷ ₹80 × 100 = 5%.
  • Biscuit brand (the OEM) receives ₹76, but has to pay for its material, making, freight and advertising from it: ₹76 ÷ ₹100 × 100 = 76% of what the customer paid.
  • Check: ₹76 + ₹4 + ₹5 + ₹15 = ₹100.

Hypothetical A ₹1,000 two-wheeler spare part

  • 3S dealer sells at ₹1,000 and buys at ₹750: ₹1,000 − ₹750 = ₹250. It keeps ₹250, which is ₹250 ÷ ₹1,000 × 100 = 25% of what the customer paid. Because it sells to the customer, this is also its margin on its own selling price.
  • Vehicle brand (the OEM) sells at ₹750 and buys at ₹480: ₹750 − ₹480 = ₹270. It earns ₹270, which is ₹270 ÷ ₹1,000 × 100 = 27% of what the customer paid. As a share of its own selling price: ₹270 ÷ ₹750 × 100 = 36%. It earns this before paying its own costs, such as making and any other material, so it is not profit.
  • Tier 1 supplier sells at ₹480 and buys at ₹200: ₹480 − ₹200 = ₹280. It earns ₹280, which is ₹280 ÷ ₹1,000 × 100 = 28% of what the customer paid. As a share of its own selling price: ₹280 ÷ ₹480 × 100 = 58.33%. It earns this before paying its own costs, such as making and any other material, so it is not profit.
  • Tier 2 supplier receives ₹200, but has to pay for its material, making and freight from it: ₹200 ÷ ₹1,000 × 100 = 20% of what the customer paid.
  • Check: ₹200 + ₹280 + ₹270 + ₹250 = ₹1,000.

Two things to notice. First, the shop or dealer closest to the customer often earns the biggest trading margin in the chain (₹15 of ₹100 for the kirana against ₹4 and ₹5 for the traders before it), but it also carries the shop, the stock and the credit. The makers' shares can be bigger, but makers also pay for material and making out of them. Second, a long chain (four steps for biscuits) leaves each trader a thin slice, which is why distributors live on volume and fast stock turns. See margins and markups and effective margin for dealers.

3. How do you move between levels?

Manoj's model In our model, businesses often climb one rung at a time, using what they learned on one rung to reach the next. The four climbs below are examples we chose to show how it could work. They are our view and are not based on statistics: we do not know how many businesses climb, or how many skip a rung. This is not the only way to read the chain, and research on global supply chains suggests that who holds the power depends on the industry as much as on the level. They are not the only ones, and the ladder's numbers are not a strict order: a licensee (Level 4) may be climbing into manufacturing at the same time.

  1. From shop to dealership: 1.2 Shop / retailer → 1.3 Wholesaler / distributor → 2.2 Authorised dealer. A shop that starts supplying other shops grows into a wholesaler. A wholesaler that has shown it can stock, sell and collect in a territory may be appointed a distributor by a company, and is then in a good position to apply to become a brand's authorised dealer.
  2. From job work to your own brand: 3.1 Job work → 3.2 Contract manufacturing → 3.5 Own brand (OEM). A job worker that buys its own material and delivers finished parts or products moves into contract manufacturing. A contract manufacturer that learns design and builds its own way to reach customers can launch its own brand.
  3. From trading to making, through a licence: 1.5 Importer / exporter → 4.1 Licensee → 3.5 Own brand (OEM). An importer that knows a product's market can license the technology to make it in India, from a laboratory, an institute or a company, Indian or foreign, and then sell it under its own brand. It needs factory skills (Level 3) as well as the licence.
  4. From your own brand to your own R&D: 3.5 Own brand (OEM) → 4.1 Licensee → 4.3 Own R&D + patents. An own-brand manufacturer can start with licensed technology, then improve it, build its own product and engineering team, and in time develop and patent technology of its own.

Not sure where you want to go? Take the self-evaluation, which suggests a level and a sub-level that fit what you want and what you can commit.

What this means for you

Manoj's model This map and the ladder are one way of looking at business, not an established theory. Where this model breaks sets out what the research says and where it disagrees.

  • Know your neighbours on the map. The businesses just before and after you decide your prices, your credit and your stock. Treat them as part of your business.
  • Follow the money back. Wherever goods move one way, money has to come back the other way. The slower it comes back, the more of your own money is stuck. See Cash before profit.
  • Watch who holds the brand. The OEM, the company whose name is on the product, sets the rules for its suppliers upstream and its dealers downstream. See OEM or commodity manufacturing for the suppliers' side and what a dealership gives and costs. An app that brings your customers does the same for a small seller: see new platform micro-businesses.
  • Ask who holds the power. At every level someone can squeeze your margin: buyers can push traders' prices down, the brand sets a dealer's terms, the brand that buys from its suppliers sets the terms, and new products face new entrants and substitutes. Porter's Five Forces is one well-known way to check. Matching each force to a level is our own example, not Porter's.
  • Climb from where you are. In our model, each level often builds on the ones below it. A new product, for example, still has to be made well (Level 3), sold through a network (Level 2), and run with cash discipline (Level 1). This is not always so: some businesses skip levels, and where this model breaks says more.

Start with the level closest to your own business: Level 1: Trader, Level 2: Dealer, Level 3: Manufacturer or Level 4: New products and services. Words you don't know? See plain words.
