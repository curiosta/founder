The model, tested against the research

Where this model breaks

The four-level ladder on this site is Manoj Sahukar's own way of thinking about business. Here is what published research says that fits it, what says otherwise, and what we could and could not read.

Manoj's model The ladder (trader, dealer, manufacturer, new products and services) is not an established theory. We searched and did not find it in the management or economics papers listed below. Treat it as a question to ask about your business, not as a law. Everything on this page about what the model says is our view; the sources are cited for what they say, in their own words as far as we could read them.

What the model says

Businesses sit on one of four levels. Each step up usually needs more money, more skill and more patience, and often gives more say over your own price. Many businesses climb by using what they learned on one rung to reach the next.

Five pieces of published work bear on that. They partly support it and partly disagree. We have not chosen the quotes to make the model look good.

What five sources say, and where they meet the model

SourceWhat it says, in plain wordsWhere it meets the modelWhat we could read
Humphrey and Schmitz (2002), How does insertion in global value chains affect upgrading in industrial clusters?, Regional Studies 36(9) Asks how much room local producers have to upgrade when they sell to large global buyers. It argues that how a cluster is inserted into a chain, and the position of developing-country firms that sell to big buyers, can enable or disable upgrading. Supports in part: moving up is a real thing firms try to do. Disagrees in part: the buyer you sell to can open or close the path, so climbing is not only a matter of your own money and effort. Abstract only, via the DOI landing page and OpenAlex. We could not open the full text.
Hobday (1998), Latecomer catch-up strategies in electronics: Samsung of Korea and ACER of Taiwan, Asia Pacific Business Review 4(2-3); and Hobday (1995), Innovation in East Asia: The Challenge to Japan, Edward Elgar Case studies of how latecomer electronics firms in South Korea and Taiwan (and, in the book, Hong Kong and Singapore) acquired technology and learned to innovate. The 1998 abstract puts forward "a simple model" of how these firms "progressed up the technological ladder" inside the subcontract system called original equipment manufacture (making to a buyer's design). Supports in part: some firms did climb, from making for others towards design and, later, their own brands. Does not show the order trader, dealer, manufacturer, new products: it describes electronics makers, not traders and dealers. Publisher's description of the book, and the 1998 paper's abstract on RePEc. We did not read either in full. Hobday is widely cited for the path OEM (make to a buyer's design), then ODM (also design), then OBM (own brand). The abstracts we opened do not use the labels ODM and OBM, so that sequence is from other writers and is not confirmed by us.
Mudambi (2008), Location, control and innovation in knowledge-intensive industries, Journal of Economic Geography 8(5) Describes two ways firms control a value chain: take on many activities yourself (vertical integration), or specialise in the creative heart of the chain and outsource the rest. It uses the mobile handset industry as its example. Disagrees in part: the paper is the source usually cited for the "smile curve", the idea that design and brand earn more than making in the middle. If that holds in an industry, making (Level 3) is not the high-value rung, and more activities do not always mean more control. Abstract only. The abstract does not use the words "smile curve", so we have not confirmed that idea from the paper itself. We are relying on how other writers describe it.
Gereffi, Humphrey and Sturgeon (2005), The governance of global value chains, Review of International Political Economy 12(1) Sets out five ways a chain can be run, from open markets to tight control by one lead firm: market, modular, relational, captive and hierarchy. Which one applies depends on how complex the product is, how easily it can be written down, and what the suppliers can do. Disagrees in part: who holds the power depends on the product and the chain. A small maker can be "captive" to one big buyer, and a trader can hold the power in a market chain. Level alone does not decide it. Abstract only.
Porter (1979), How competitive forces shape strategy, Harvard Business Review, March-April Argues that five forces shape how competitive an industry is: new entrants, customers, suppliers, substitutes and rivals. Knowing them helps a company take a position that is less open to attack. Disagrees in part: profit depends on the structure of the industry as much as on your rung. A maker in a hard industry can earn less than a trader in a good one. Our "matters most at each level" table on the Five Forces page is our own mapping, not Porter's. The publisher's summary page only; the full article is behind a paywall.

Checked on 25 October 2026. The publisher pages for the three journal papers (Taylor & Francis and Oxford University Press) sat behind a security check that our tools could not pass, so we read each abstract through OpenAlex and Crossref records, which republish the publisher's abstract; we have not seen the publisher's own page. For Porter we opened the HBR summary page, for Hobday the Edward Elgar page and the RePEc abstract. Where we write "abstract only" we did not read the full paper and cannot say what it concludes beyond its abstract.

Where the model breaks, in our reading

Manoj's model These are our reading of the sources above and of the cases on this site. They are not findings of the papers.

  1. A higher level does not always mean more control or more profit. If design and brand are where value sits in an industry, a retailer with its own brand or a design firm can do better than a factory. And a big buyer can hold a factory on a tight leash. See OEM or commodity manufacturing.
  2. The order is not a path. Some businesses start at Level 3 or 4 and were never traders. Others stay as traders for good reasons. We have no figures on how many climb, skip or stay, so the "common climbs" on our maps are examples, not statistics.
  3. Someone else can hold the power at any level. A platform can set a seller's price and a brand can set a dealer's terms. See new platform micro-businesses and what a dealership gives and costs.
  4. Staying where you are can be a good business. A well-run trader or dealer that turns its money quickly can beat a poorly run factory. Climbing is a choice, not a goal for everyone.
  5. The numbers 1 to 4 and the sub-level order are our judgement. Drop shipping is placed first (1.1) because the model starts from the least money needed. It is the model's anchor, not a law, and not advice that it is a good place to start. Drop shipping often has thin margins and a high rate of shops closing; see Plain words for the figures and their limits. The words we use for money, risk and control are not measured.
  6. The self-evaluation measures what you want, not what will work. It points to a level to aim for, based on this model. It has not been tested against how businesses actually do. See the self-evaluation.

Why keep the model, then?

Manoj's view is that a simple ladder is a useful first way to ask where your money comes from and who can squeeze it. A model can be useful without being true everywhere. The research above is a reason to hold it loosely: use it to ask questions, then check the answers against your own industry.
