TRIZ: solving a business problem by removing the trade-off

TRIZ in plain words: name the trade-off, picture the ideal result, use what you have, then try 40 prompts. Evidence is thin; where to practise is listed.

By Manoj Sahukar · 26 October 2026 · 20 min read

Where it comes from. TRIZ is a Russian acronym, usually translated “theory of inventive problem solving”. It was started by Genrich Altshuller, a Soviet patent reviewer, who began this work between 1946 and 1949. His first publication, written with Shapiro, came in 1956, and the name TRIZ was introduced in 1970 (Souchkov, “A Brief History of TRIZ”, written by a TRIZ consultant, so read it as an insider’s account). It was built for engineering, not business. The international TRIZ association, MATRIZ, defines it in its glossary as an applied science of development directions and “methods for improvement of engineering systems”. Using it on business problems came later, in work such as Mann and Domb’s 1999 article, which this page uses for its examples.

What it is, in plain words

TRIZ is a way of thinking about a problem that has a trade-off in it: you want more of one good thing, but getting it makes another good thing worse. Its idea is that, instead of settling for a middle compromise, you ask whether the trade-off can be removed.

Two of its parts are used on this page:

  • A contradiction. The official glossary calls an engineering contradiction a “parametric model of the problem”: an attempt to improve one parameter of the system “leads to worsening of another parameter”. In plain words: “I want X, but X makes Y worse.” A physical contradiction is when you need one thing to be two opposite things at once, for example “the shop must stock a lot (for choice) and stock very little (to save cash)”.
  • The 40 “inventive principles”. The glossary calls an inventive principle an “abstract model of a solution” that gives “generalized recommendations for modifying the system”. In plain words: a list of 40 common ways that people have got out of a trade-off, each a prompt such as “split it into parts” or “do part of the work beforehand”. In engineering TRIZ there is also a table (the contradiction matrix) that suggests which of the 40 to try for a given trade-off. For business there is no such table that we found. Mann and Domb wrote in 1999 that “as yet at least, there is no business version of the Contradiction Matrix”, and that their list “exists merely to stimulate creative thinking about business situations”. It is not meant to replace detailed analysis.

A small example of a contradiction

Hypothetical A kirana owner finds that customers keep asking for brands she does not stock. More choice is good for sales. But every extra brand ties up cash in stock that may sit on the shelf, and takes shelf space. That is the contradiction: “more choice makes my cash and shelf worse.”

Say the ideal result is: “customers find what they want, and I hold no extra stock.” Next list what she already has: a distributor who visits regularly, a phone with her regular customers on it, a spare shelf at the back, a counter where people wait. Then look at the principles. “Preliminary Action” (do part of the work before it is needed) might suggest taking an order from a regular customer before buying the stock. “Intermediary” might suggest asking the distributor to hold the slower brands. These are our own illustrations of how to think. They are not research findings, and we do not know whether either would work for any real shop.

A small manufacturer can find the same pattern: shorter delivery time against cash tied up in finished goods. The same four steps below apply.

The 40 principles, 12 of them in business terms

The only business version of the list we opened is Mann and Domb’s 1999 article. They went through management books and articles and sorted the ideas they found under the 40 principles. The examples below are theirs, quoted or closely paraphrased, and they are examples that fit a principle after the fact. Mann and Domb do not claim that TRIZ produced any of them. Examples are 1990s ones, as reported in 1999. The “plain meaning” column is our own wording.

Principle (name as in the article) Plain meaning (ours) Example in the article
Segmentation Split something into independent parts, or into smaller parts “Franchise outlets”; “Flexible pensions”; “Use of temporary workers on short-term projects”
Local quality Make each part suit its own conditions, instead of one uniform way “Franchise fast food outlets have local dishes in addition to normal product range”
Universality Let one thing do several jobs “One-stop shopping - supermarkets sell insurance, banking services, fuel, newspapers, etc.”
Preliminary Action Do part of the job before it is needed Benetton’s “retarded differentiation”: clothing is knitted before it is dyed, and colour is applied only when the season’s popular colours emerge
‘The Other Way Round’ Reverse the usual direction, or move what is usually fixed “Home-shopping”; “Home banking”
Dynamics Make something rigid adjustable “Cafeteria benefits - where employees pick which types of insurance, health system, etc. they want”
Another Dimension Look at the problem from another side, or change what you sell Interface, the carpet maker, offered an “Evergreen Lease”: customers “just pay a monthly service fee that guarantees they will always have clean, attractive carpets”
Continuity of Useful Action Remove idle time “Hot-tilling in supermarkets”: staff do other tasks in quiet periods and move to the tills when queues build
“Blessing in Disguise” Turn a harm into a benefit “Loss-leader strategy for increasing sales”; “Restrict supply of goods to create scarcity value”
Feedback Use measurements or customer response to adjust “(Supermarket) loyalty cards - provide customer shopping profile information”
‘Intermediary’ Put a go-between or a temporary helper in the middle “Sub-contract non-core business (e.g. cleaning services, transport)”; “Use bridging loan arrangements to help cashflow”
Self-service Let the thing serve itself, or use waste and spare capacity “Body Shop re-cycles used containers brought back by customers”; “Re-hire retired workers for jobs where their experience is needed”

The article lists all 40 and says that in the texts it examined it found no idea that would need a 41st. The authors also say they were “not claiming the search of management texts for this article was in any way comprehensive”. That is the authors’ own claim about their own sorting, not a test. We picked the 12 that are easiest to picture in a shop or small factory; the article also gives business readings of the other 28.

How to use it: four steps

These steps are our short version of what TRIZ teachers describe (the ideal result and resources steps come from Apte’s course notes at IIT Bombay, and from the Mann and Domb article, which advises formulating “the ideal final result and the reasons that the ideal cannot be achieved”).

  1. Name the contradiction. Write one sentence: “I want ___, but ___ makes ___ worse.” If you cannot name two goals pulling against each other, this is not a TRIZ problem (see “When not to use it”).
  2. State the ideal result. Describe the perfect outcome as if cost and effort were free. The glossary defines the ideal final result as a solution where “the problem is fully eliminated with minimal changes to the system”. It defines ideality as the benefits divided by the costs and harms (“payment factors”), and says those include more than money: “space, environmental damage, maintenance, etc.”
  3. List the resources you already have. People, time, space, stock, information, customers, money that is idle, waste. Apte’s notes list seven kinds of resource: space, time, system, function, information, substances, and energy and field. For a shop, read them as plain words (shelf, hours, staff, the sales book, goods, power, and what customers already do).
  4. Look at which principles fit. Go through the 12 above, or the full 40 in the article, in random order. Mann and Domb suggest this to “keep your thinking independent”. Pick two or three that make you think of something new, and test the idea cheaply before you spend money.

What the evidence says: not proven

TRIZ is a set of habits that some teachers and engineers find useful. It is not proven to give better results. Please read this section before you rely on it.

  • It was not scientifically validated. In a 2021 paper in Design Science (the paper is about how people pick principles), Borgianni, Fiorineschi, Frillici and Rotini write that TRIZ “has not been developed following the standard procedures of scientific validation”. They add that the reliability of the matrix is “often questioned”, and that an agreement on “a solid and reliable procedure” for choosing principles “is far from being reached”.
  • No study we found shows that TRIZ improves business results. A 2016 critical survey of more than 200 published industrial case studies (Spreafico and Russo, Procedia CIRP) found that a “real successful case study” showing how TRIZ contributed “is missing”. It also says the spread of TRIZ “has never reached the level of capillarity expected”. Their cases are engineering or industrial. Published case studies are chosen by the people who wrote them, so they lean towards success stories.
  • A test of the engineering matrix gave a mixed result. Darrell Mann, one of TRIZ’s best-known authors, had 32 students check the matrix against more than 130 mechanical patents, chosen by the students, with a lean towards “interesting” ones. He reported that the matrix would have pointed to the principles the inventor used in 48% of cases, and concluded that it is “still relevant for mechanical problems”. That is a test of engineering, by an insider, with a hand-picked sample. It says nothing directly about shops or factories.
  • Users report hurdles. Ilevbare, Probert and Phaal surveyed people who had tried TRIZ “successfully and unsuccessfully” (Technovation, 2013; we read only the abstract). They report challenges “from the complex nature of the methodology to underlying organisational and cultural issues”. That is self-reported experience, not a measure of results.
  • For business use, the evidence is thinner still. The Borgianni sample of 161 solutions from 53 conference papers is a convenience sample. Of these, 44 were “human-related” (management, administration, society, health, education) and 127 were technical; in the human-related ones, “Segmentation” and “Parameter changes” were used most. That shows what TRIZ users chose, not what worked.
  • Advocacy is not evidence. Some TRIZ bodies and trainers say that it “works” or is “proven”, or quote large companies without a source. We did not use any such claim. One TRIZ association website we opened calls TRIZ “a proven problem solving tool”. We list no result on its authority.

So, in short: TRIZ is a structured way of asking “what if I did not have to choose?”. Treat the principles as prompts to think with, and test the ideas you get.

When not to use it

  • When you have no trade-off. If the problem is simply “I need more money” or “I do not know what customers want”, find out the facts first. TRIZ starts from a trade-off you can name.
  • When the question is about customers’ needs. Start with Maslow’s hierarchy of needs or a talk with ten customers (see our suggestion below).
  • As proof for a decision. The 12 examples fit the principles after the fact. They do not show that the principle will work for you.
  • When someone sells you a certificate as the answer. Certification is a paid credential. We found no study linking certification to business results.
  • For a serious engineering or safety problem. The tools were built for engineering and the full method takes training. This page is a taste, not a method.
  • To label your level or your business. TRIZ is a thinking tool, not a test of where you stand.

Pairing it with other pages

Manoj’s view TRIZ is worth having on this site as a source of ideas for business, and the site should be planned around it. He asked for that. It is his direction, not a research finding. He wants it linked to the four levels: Level 1: Trader, Level 2: Dealer, Level 3: Manufacturer and Level 4: New products. No source we opened applies TRIZ to those four levels, because the ladder is Manoj’s own model (where it breaks). We have not claimed any particular contradiction at any level.

Our suggestion (not a finding from any source): use the two pages together. Maslow’s question (“what need does this purchase serve?”) can help you describe the ideal result in step 2. TRIZ then helps you look for a way to get that result without paying a price somewhere else.

Practise TRIZ

This is a sourced list as of 26 October 2026. It is not every resource that exists. We opened each page below on that date. We give a price only where the page showed one, and we note any link we could not open fully. Almost all TRIZ material is written for engineers, and most of it is in English. Many providers earn money from TRIZ training and certification, so read their claims with that in mind.

Official bodies and free material

  • MATRIZ (International TRIZ Association), run by the association itself. matriz.org and certification page. News, events and a five-level certification (L1 to L3 practitioner, L4 specialist, L5 master). Cost: no exam fee was shown on the certification page; it showed USD 20 for a duplicate certificate. Only accredited representatives certify L1 to L3, and the list on the page includes one in India who lists Hindi and English. Language and level: English; beginner to expert. Bias: it is the body that issues the certificates; the figure it gives for people certified is its own claim.
  • MATRIZ Knowledge Base, run by MATRIZ. wiki.matriz.org. A glossary, an interactive contradiction matrix and a form for the ARIZ method. Cost: free (no price on the pages). Language and level: English; beginner to intermediate. Bias: the pages say they do not replace formal training; the matrix is the engineering one, not a business one.
  • The TRIZ Journal, a long-running TRIZ magazine. the-trizjournal.com; the old archive is mirrored at metodolog.ru. Free articles, including Mann’s. Language and level: English; beginner to advanced. Bias: articles are by consultants and trainers; the newest posts we saw on the site are from 2020.
  • Altshuller Institute for TRIZ Studies, a US body. aitriz.org. Membership and certification. Cost (the page is undated; seen 26 Oct 2026): the Associate test costs $150 for annual members, or $250 including membership; the Specialist certification costs $800. Language and level: English; its Associate test is open-book and covers engineering topics such as the matrix and the laws of system evolution. Bias: it sells membership and certificates; it says it had “several years of reduced activity” and is “re-energizing”, and its site makes claims such as “proven” that we did not use.
  • ETRIA, the European TRIZ Association. etria.eu. Lists the TRIZ Future conferences and TRAI 2026 (21 to 23 October 2026, in Tunisia; the dates have just passed). Cost: not checked. Language and level: English; mainly for practitioners and researchers. Bias: it is a community of trainers and researchers.

Courses and online lessons

  • Coursera: “Creative Thinking: Techniques and Tools for Success”, Imperial College London (Peter Childs). link. TRIZ is Module 5 of 7 (about three hours, including the contradiction matrix); the course also covers other creativity tools such as SCAMPER. Cost: the page shows no price; a paid certificate and financial aid are mentioned. Language and level: English (subtitles in many languages); beginner. Bias: TRIZ is one module, and includes an interview with an Oxford TRIZ consultant.
  • Coursera: “Systematic Creative Thinking: Tools for Success”, Imperial College London. link. TRIZ in weeks 3 and 4, including a reading, “Extending the use of TRIZ to non-technical challenges”. Cost: no price shown on the page; part of a Specialization. Language and level: English; intermediate. Bias: as above.
  • NPTEL: “Functional and Conceptual Design”, IIT Madras (Prof. T. Asokan). Outline on Class Central, which lists week 11 as “Logical Method- TRIZ” and the course as “Not Currently Offered”. The NPTEL page itself did not load for us (link), so we could not open it fully. Cost: Class Central calls it a free course. Language and level: English; for undergraduate or graduate engineering students. Bias: TRIZ is one week of a product-design course.
  • Prakash R. Apte’s course notes, IIT Bombay. Introduction to TRIZ and a 5-day course outline. Free to read; the pages are undated and appear old. They include a section on “How will TRIZ help in INDIA?”. Language and level: English; beginner to intermediate; engineering. Bias: one practitioner’s course notes; the 5-day page makes strong claims about TRIZ (“apparently the only scientifically based systematic methodology”) that we did not use, and the two pages give different patent counts.
  • Oxford Creativity (Oxford TRIZ), a UK training company founded by Karen Gadd. triz.co.uk and its free webinars; it also has a YouTube channel (we saw the channel exists, and did not watch its videos). It sells a paid online course and in-house training. Cost: the site lists on-demand webinars and no price was shown for them; the online course is paid (about 10 hours over 5 days, 7 modules) and its price was not shown. Language and level: English; beginner to intermediate. Bias: this is a vendor, and its site makes marketing claims, such as that its approach has “never failed”, which we did not use.

In India

  • TRIZTI (also called ATTI), a non-profit trust in Bengaluru. trizti.org. It says it is India’s regional MATRIZ association and runs Bharat TRIZ Utsav (six editions so far) and workshops. MATRIZ’s news page announced TRIZ Level 1 in Bengaluru (28 to 30 April 2026) and Level 2 online (May 2026); both are past. Cost: not shown. Language and level: English; all levels. Bias: its goals (1 million inventors by 2030) are aims, not results.
  • Proinn Consultancy, a commercial training firm in Bengaluru. proinnconsultancy.com. Courses from TRIZ Level 1 to Level 3 (Level 3 is a 10-day workshop). Cost: paid, price not shown. Language and level: English; beginner to advanced. Bias: it is a seller of training and shows testimonials; it says its Level 1 is certified by MATRIZ, which we did not check beyond the MATRIZ accreditation list.

We also opened the site of the “TRIZ Association of Asia” (trizassociation.org). It mixes template filler text with TRIZ text and calls TRIZ “proven”, so we do not list it as a resource.

Books (publisher or library records)

  • Karen Gadd, TRIZ for Engineers: Enabling Inventive Problem Solving, Wiley, 2011, 512 pages. Wiley page. Cost: the e-book showed $72.00 on 26 October 2026. English; for engineers. Bias: the author runs Oxford Creativity; the page is promotional.
  • Darrell Mann, Hands-On Systematic Innovation for Business and Management, IFR Press, 2004, 539 pages. Known from a 2004 TRIZ Journal review by Michael Slocum, which says its focus is TRIZ in non-technical areas. The review gave a 2004 price of £40; we did not see a current price. English. Bias: the author is a TRIZ consultant; publishers’ names differ between sources.
  • Genrich Altshuller and Igor Altov, And Suddenly the Inventor Appeared, Technical Innovation Center, 1996 (the founder’s book for general readers). A library catalogue record (the Open Library search service) confirms it; the book’s own page was blocked to us. Apte’s course notes give 1994 for it.
  • Savransky, Engineering of Creativity, CRC Press, 2000. Publisher page. An engineering text; the publisher’s text is promotional.

Limits and criticisms

  • TRIZ is not proven to improve business results. Its own scholars say it was not validated in the usual scientific way, and that the evidence of what it contributed in case studies “is missing”. See “What the evidence says”.
  • It was built from engineering patents. The official definition says “engineering systems”. Using it on shops, dealers or factories is an extension. The business examples above are collected after the fact from management writing, not from tests.
  • Counts of how many patents Altshuller studied differ between sources. Apte’s introduction page says 200,000 patents and his 5-day course page says 400,000. Savransky (TRIZ Journal) says about 40,000 “strong” patents were taken from about 400,000. We give no single number. Zlotin and Zusman (a TRIZ vendor) also report two different splits of inventions into five “levels” (Altshuller’s own count and a later one), and write that the levels “were not used for practical problem-solving purposes”.
  • The “40 principles” are a list from patents, not a law. Souchkov, a TRIZ consultant, writes that the matrix and 40 principles are “still the most popular TRIZ tools; although their applicability is limited”.
  • We read some sources in part. Ilevbare and colleagues: the abstract only. Souchkov, Savransky, Mann and Domb, Mann (2002), Zlotin and Zusman, Spreafico and Russo, Borgianni and colleagues, Apte: opened as web pages or PDFs (the Spreafico and Russo copy was a free academic-site copy).
  • We have no Indian business example with a source. We did not find one we could open, so we give none. A paper on TRIZ at Reliance Industries (Ohler, Shahani and Borde, 2016) is a lead we did not open.
  • We did not compare TRIZ with other methods. Other idea-generating tools exist (the Coursera course lists SCAMPER next to TRIZ), but we have no opened source that tests one against another.
  • The Practise list will date. Prices, events and links change. Re-check before you pay.

Search: web searches, publisher and library records, the TRIZ Journal archive and MATRIZ pages, checked 26 October 2026. No result is not proof that none exists.
