Frameworks, in plain words

Eight standard business frameworks explained simply and applied to Indian traders, dealers, manufacturers and new-product founders, with worked examples.

A framework is a checklist that many people use: a way of making sure you look at the whole picture before you decide. Being widely used is not the same as being proven to work, and each page has a short “Limits and criticisms” box. None of them gives you the answer. Each one helps you ask better questions about your business and about the rung you want to climb to.

Which one when

  • Why do my customers buy? Maslow’s hierarchy of needs, five kinds of human need, used as a prompt about what a purchase is for. It is first because Manoj sees it as the most basic one (his view); the list is organised by question, not as a sequence. The research does not support a strict ladder, and the page says so.
  • Who can squeeze my margin? Porter’s Five Forces, a well-known way to check who holds the power in an industry. Matching the forces to the levels of our ladder is our own example, not Porter’s.
  • What do I want to build? SOAR, which starts from your strengths and aspirations (using it for “which level do I want” is our application). There is a short SOAR worksheet on the self-evaluation result.
  • How do I get from here to there? GAP analysis, comparing today’s sub-level with your target, rung by rung.
  • Where do I stand, and what should I do about it? SWOT, then TOWS. TOWS turns the four SWOT boxes into moves.
  • What is changing around me? PESTLE: politics, the economy, society, technology, law and the environment, including GST, licences and policy.
  • Is my organisation ready for a bigger business? McKinsey 7-S. Using it as a readiness check before a climb is our own application, not the authors’.
  • What should I fix this week? NOISE, a quick weekly operations check.

How we write these. Each page keeps the framework’s standard definition and explains it in our own words; we do not copy the originators’ text. We credit an originator only after checking it in the original paper, the publisher’s page or a scholarly history, and we link that source. Where the origin is disputed (as with SWOT) we say so, and where we could not verify an origin (as with GAP analysis and NOISE) we say that plainly. Every number in an example is labelled Hypothetical and checked by script.
