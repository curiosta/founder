Maslow's hierarchy of needs: a prompt, not a ladder

Maslow's five kinds of need in plain words, what the research says about them, and how to use them as a question about why your customer buys.

By Manoj Sahukar · 26 October 2026 · 12 min read

Where it comes from. The American psychologist Abraham Maslow set out the idea in “A Theory of Human Motivation”, published in the journal Psychological Review in 1943 (the paper, reprinted by York University’s Classics in the History of Psychology). The pyramid you usually see is not in that paper. In a 2019 article, the management scholars Todd Bridgman, Stephen Cummings and John Ballard write that “Maslow never created a pyramid to represent the hierarchy of needs”. In an interview about the article (the same authors, so not an independent check) they say they found no pyramid in his books, articles, diaries or archived papers. The earliest one they found is in a 1960 article by Charles McDermid in Business Horizons, and they add: “If there is an earlier pyramid, we did not find it.”

What it is

Maslow’s idea is that people have several kinds of need, and that the one pressing hardest at any moment depends on what is missing. His own example: “man lives by bread alone”, but only “when there is no bread”. When food is plentiful, he wrote, “other (and ‘higher’) needs emerge and these, rather than physiological hungers, dominate the organism.” He called this a hierarchy of “relative prepotency”: some needs shout louder than others until they are met.

For a founder it is useful for one reason. It reminds you that one product can serve very different needs, and that “why does this person spend money on this?” is a better first question than “what does this person earn?”

The five levels, in plain words

These are the five groups Maslow described in 1943, with his own words where we quote them.

  1. Body needs (he called them physiological): food, water, sleep, warmth.
  2. Safety: feeling secure. His examples include “a job with tenure and protection”, “a savings account” and insurance “of various kinds”.
  3. Belonging and love: friends, family, being part of a group. He wrote of “the love and affection and belongingness needs”.
  4. Esteem: respect from others and self-respect: “strength, … achievement, … adequacy, … confidence”, and also “reputation or prestige”, recognition and importance.
  5. Self-actualisation: becoming what you are capable of. In his words, “the desire to become more and more what one is, to become everything that one is capable of becoming.”

A sixth level is sometimes added. The psychologist Mark Koltko-Rivera argues in a 2006 paper that late in his life Maslow put self-transcendence (motives that go beyond the self) above self-actualisation. Koltko-Rivera himself notes that this reading has been objected to. The five-level list above is the version most people know.

What the research says: a loose guide, not a strict ladder

  • Maslow did not claim it was rigid. In the same 1943 paper he wrote that the order “is not nearly as rigid as we may have implied”, and listed exceptions: in some people esteem seems more important than love; some innately creative people create “in spite of lack of basic satisfaction”; and people who have lived for long at a very low level (his example is chronic unemployment) may stay content if they can get enough food. The paper reasons mainly from his own clinical and everyday observations (“It derives most directly … from clinical experience”), not from a survey. On culture he wrote: “No claim is made that it is ultimate or universal for all cultures.” He claimed only that it is “relatively more ultimate, more universal, more basic” than surface differences such as hairstyle, clothes or tastes in food.
  • Tests of a strict order mostly do not find one. In 1976, Mahmoud Wahba and Lawrence Bridwell reviewed ten factor-analytic and three ranking studies and found “only partial support for the concept of need hierarchy”. Studies that followed people over time showed no support for the idea that meeting one need switches on the next. They also called the theory “almost a nontestable theory”, partly because Maslow’s defence rested on “logical as well as clinical insight rather than well-developed research findings”.
  • More recent studies point the same way, but say needs still matter. Louis Tay and Ed Diener studied people in 123 countries (2011). They found that meeting needs was consistently linked to well-being, and that each need’s link to well-being was “largely independent of whether other needs were fulfilled”, which is not what a strict ladder predicts. They also found the ordering for psychosocial needs “fairly consistent across country conditions”, while the meeting of basic and safety needs was “contingent on country membership”. A 2023 study of a large representative Mexican database (Rojas, Méndez and Watkins-Fassler) tested four assumptions often attached to Maslow’s theory (that needs are met in sequence, that income matters for meeting them, that the order of the levels matches how much each adds to well-being, and that Maslow’s path is the best one) and reported that “the four assumptions are rejected”. It highlights belonging and esteem, and a balanced strategy across needs.
  • Some researchers defend the basic idea. Douglas Kenrick and colleagues (2010) argue that “the basic foundational structure of the pyramid is worth preserving”, while adding extensions drawn from evolutionary theory. So the debate is about how strict and how universal it is, not about whether needs differ in kind.
  • Culture and individualism. The 2019 authors note that the theory has been criticised for “promoting an elitist, individualistic view of management”. Koltko-Rivera links the self-actualisation level to an individual-centred outlook and the individualism-versus-collectivism difference between cultures. A separate, general warning from Joseph Henrich, Steven Heine and Ara Norenzayan (2010) is that behavioural scientists routinely publish broad claims about human psychology from samples drawn entirely from Western, educated, industrialised, rich and democratic (“WEIRD”) societies, while results vary substantially across populations. That paper is not about Maslow. We cite it only as a reason to be careful when a theory built in one place is used in another.

In short: treat the five levels as five kinds of need that people often have at the same time. Do not treat them as a staircase that every customer climbs in the same order.

How to use it as a prompt

The one question this framework gives a founder is: what need does my customer’s purchase serve? Then ask three more:

  1. Is it one need or several? The same purchase can serve more than one at once.
  2. Who else is the purchase for? A gift, a festival purchase or something bought to be seen can be about other people’s view of the buyer.
  3. What would the customer drop first if money got tight? Do not assume the answer. Ask a few customers.

Hypothetical A kirana sells loose dal and a boxed premium gift pack of sweets. For the dal, the question may lead you to the body-needs end: the buyer wants it cheap, available and on credit. For the gift pack, the question may lead you towards belonging or esteem: the buyer wants to give something that looks right. That could change what you stock at the counter, how you wrap it and when you promote it. These two cases are our own illustrations of how to ask the question. They are not research findings and we have no data on what any buyer feels.

The answer to your question is a guess until you have checked it with customers. Treat Maslow as the way you form the guess, not as evidence for it.

Reading the consumer-data layer with it

The India by place section reads government household spending data. The numbers from the Ministry of Statistics’ fact sheet on the 2023-24 Household Consumption Expenditure Survey show one way to use the prompt. Food was 47.04% of average monthly spending per person in rural India and 39.68% in urban India in 2023-24, against 52.90% and 42.62% in 2011-12. The survey’s “food” group includes beverages, refreshments and processed food (9.84% of spending in rural India and 11.09% in urban India in 2023-24), so it is not only staples.

Use that as a way of reading, not a rule. A spending share tells you where money goes. It does not tell you which need a person feels, and it does not put any place at a “level” of Maslow’s scheme. We make no such claim about any state, district or city. The data come from one agency, so check anything you plan to rely on at your own counter.

When not to use it

  • To sort whole places or groups of customers into levels. The research above does not support a fixed order, and it would turn a prompt into a stereotype.
  • To predict what one customer will buy next. It is not a forecasting tool, and its tests are about well-being and satisfaction, not purchases.
  • As a reason for a price or a product. Test the price or product with customers.
  • To explain your staff’s motivation with a single level. People care about several needs at once.
  • To carry a result from one country, or one community, to another. The evidence is mixed across cultures.

Manoj’s view I think of Maslow’s hierarchy as the most basic framework to start with, which is why it is first in this list. That is my judgement about where to begin, not a finding from research. I have not tied it to the four levels of this site’s ladder, and I am not claiming any such link.

Limits and criticisms

  • The pyramid is a later picture. Maslow’s 1943 paper has none. The 2019 authors trace the earliest pyramid they found to 1960, and most criticism, they say, is aimed at a simplified version popularised by Douglas McGregor.
  • Evidence for a strict order is weak. The 1976 review found only partial support, and the 2011 and 2023 studies we cite do not support a fixed sequence. Kenrick and colleagues argue that the basic structure is still worth keeping.
  • The studies measure well-being or satisfaction, not buying. None of the studies we opened tests what Indian customers buy. The Mexican study covers one country.
  • We read some sources only in part. Tay and Diener and Rojas and colleagues: abstracts only. Wahba and Bridwell: the abstract and the concluding discussion. Kenrick and colleagues: the abstract only. Koltko-Rivera: opened in full (an author-hosted copy).
  • A disputed claim we cannot settle. A 2014 blog post by Karen Lincoln Michel (here) reports Professor Cindy Blackstock saying at a conference that Maslow’s hierarchy was borrowed from Blackfoot teachings, in which, as the post reports it, self-actualisation is the foundation and community and “cultural perpetuity” come after. The 2019 authors say that Maslow wrote briefly about his fieldwork with the Blackfoot people and that nothing suggests he borrowed ideas from them; that is their view in an interview. We did not find a primary source that settles it either way, so we take no side. If the speaker’s account is right, it is an example of a culture ordering needs differently; we have not been able to check it.
  • What we left out. We do not state Maslow’s later additions beyond self-transcendence, because we did not open a source for them. We do not use Hofstede’s work on cultural differences, because we could not open it.
  • Using it for Indian small businesses is our own application. Maslow did not write about them.
