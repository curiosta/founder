PESTLE: scanning the changes around your business

Political, economic, social, technological, legal and environmental change, from GST and licences to policy and tech rules, scanned for your level.

By Manoj Sahukar · 12 October 2026 · 4 min read

Where it comes from: no single originator. The usual starting point is Francis J. Aguilar’s book Scanning the Business Environment (Macmillan, 1967; Open Library record), which sorted the outside world into economic, technical, political and social sectors, sometimes abbreviated ETPS. Those four were later rearranged as STEP and PEST (a history of the acronyms on a UCLA faculty page credits Arnold Brown of the Institute of Life Insurance with STEP), and legal and environmental factors were added to make PESTLE or PESTEL. We could not verify who first added them. A recent working paper by Richard Puyt and Dag Øivind Madsen, “The origins of PEST analysis”, argues that PEST grew out of teaching and consulting rather than one author.

What it is

PESTLE is a checklist of six kinds of change outside your business:

  • Political: government policy, schemes, trade policy, state-level rules.
  • Economic: demand, inflation, interest rates, the exchange rate, the monsoon’s effect on rural spending.
  • Social: how people live, shop and pay; population and tastes.
  • Technological: new ways to make, sell, pay and deliver.
  • Legal: laws and the licences, registrations and standards you must meet.
  • Environmental: pollution, waste and packaging rules, and climate.

You can’t control any of them. The point is to see them early enough to prepare.

When to use it

  • Before you enter a new market, import or export a product, or launch a new product.
  • Once a year, and around the key business dates such as the Union Budget, when policy changes cluster.
  • As the “opportunities and threats” input to SWOT and TOWS.

What to look at in India, by letter

Letter Examples to check Where it bites most
Political Union Budget announcements, production-linked incentive schemes, trade policy, state industrial policies Level 4, importers and exporters (1.5)
Economic Interest rates on working-capital loans, the rupee exchange rate, rural demand after the monsoon All levels, especially stock-heavy dealers
Social Digital payments, buying on apps, the festive season Traders, new platform micro-businesses
Technological Online marketplaces, ONDC, automation in factories Manufacturers, middlemen
Legal GST rates and returns (set by the GST Council), an Importer-Exporter Code for importers and exporters, food licences, BIS standards, data-protection law Level 4, 1.5
Environmental Plastic packaging and e-waste rules, pollution-control consents for factories Manufacturers, Level 4

Worked example: an importer and a duty change

Hypothetical An importer (1.5) when the duty on a product doubles

An importer brings in 1,000 units with a value of ₹10,00,000 and sells them at ₹1,500 each. The duty rate is 10% and rises to 20%. (The rates are made up; real rates depend on the product. GST is left out because a registered importer can usually claim the GST paid on imports back as input tax credit.)

  • Duty at 10%: ₹10,00,000 × 10% = ₹1,00,000
  • Duty at 20%: ₹10,00,000 × 20% = ₹2,00,000
  • Cost per unit before: (₹10,00,000 + ₹1,00,000) ÷ 1,000 = ₹1,100
  • Cost per unit after: (₹10,00,000 + ₹2,00,000) ÷ 1,000 = ₹1,200
  • Gross margin before: (₹1,500 − ₹1,100) ÷ ₹1,500 × 100 = 26.67%
  • Gross margin after: (₹1,500 − ₹1,200) ÷ ₹1,500 × 100 = 20%

A change announced on one day can cut a quarter of the importer’s margin. A PESTLE scan before the Budget would put “duty change” on the list, with a plan: raise the price, find a local supplier, or hold less stock.

Level 4: new products

New products meet more of PESTLE than any other level: a new product may need a licence (food, drugs, medical devices), certification to Indian standards, data-protection compliance for an app, approval for a new technology, or a GST classification nobody has tested. Before you build, list each letter and ask “what rule or change could stop us selling?” See technology transfer in India and the new products level.

Common mistakes

  • Listing everything. Keep the two or three changes that would really move your sales or costs, and plan for those.
  • No dates. Policy changes cluster around the Budget and GST Council meetings; put them in your calendar.
  • Mixing up political and legal. Political is the direction of policy; legal is the rule you must follow today.
  • Doing it once. Rules change every year. Update the scan at least yearly.

Related: Porter’s Five Forces, since outside changes often shift who holds the power.

Limits and criticisms

  • A simple checklist. A 2026 systematic review in Futures notes that PESTEL “has been criticised in the field of strategic management for being a simplistic checklist that lacks rigorous analytical depth”, while also saying it has gained prominence because of its simplicity and use in teaching (DOI, abstract only).
  • No single standard version. The same review lists dozens of variants of the acronym (PEST, PESTLE, PESTEL, STEEP and others), so there is no agreed list of factors.
  • Listing is not analysis. A PESTLE list tells you what is changing, not which change matters most or what to do. That judgement is yours. We found no study that tests whether doing a PESTLE improves results for small firms.
